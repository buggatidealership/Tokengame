How we keep score

The rules, printed in the front of the book. They are the same every round.

01

The call

The day before a company reports, every model gets the same short question. No list of factors, no hints. It can use any tool it likes. It answers up or down, and by how much, and the call goes in the book before the print.

The shape of the prompt
02

The grade

After the print, the market answers. Each call is marked right way or wrong way, and we write down how many points it missed by.

03

How a round is won

Call the direction right, then land closest to the real move. A wrong-way call can't win, however close the number.

04

Ties

If two right-way calls land exactly as close, both win the round.

05

Nobody wins

If every call goes the wrong way, nobody wins. The round still counts, and the book shows it as big as any win.

06

The self-review

After the grade, each model gets its own call back and works out what it overweighted and what it underweighted. Nobody tells it what went wrong. It carries its own notes into the next call. Same question, a little more experience.

Step 1

Up or down: do they get it right?

How often each model has called the direction right so far. A coin flip gets 50% over time. Learning looks like lines that climb away from 50% and stay up there.

Step 2

How far off were they?

Each bar is one call: how many percentage points it missed the real move by. Shorter is better. Learning looks like bars that shrink.

    p. 4

    Margin notes

    The questions a careful reader writes in the margin. Our answers, plainly.

    Why one short question?
    One question. Full freedom.

    Tell a model what to look at and it only looks there. We'd rather it find its own way in. The prompt only starts it. The learning happens between rounds, in the self-review.

    Is this the right way?
    Honestly? Nobody knows.

    Nobody has figured out how to get a machine to read an earnings print. Our bet is that freedom beats instructions. One recent study found that elaborate forecasting prompts don't help and sometimes hurt. That's a hint, not proof.

    Why is the one with the most tools last?
    Different rigs.

    Same prompt, different setups. GPT and Kimi run in chat workspaces. Claude runs as an agent with the most tools and data.

    So what now?
    Time will tell.

    Until the record is long enough to mean something, we stop talking and make the next call.

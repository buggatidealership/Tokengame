The analytics desk

Two questions, in order. First: do they get the direction right? Only then does the second one matter: how close do they get? The charts grow with every print.

Step 1

Up or down: do they get it right?

Share of calls in the right direction so far, round by round. A coin flip lands on 50% over time. Learning looks like a line that climbs away from the dashed line and stays there.

Step 2

Right way. How close?

When the direction was right, how many points off the call was. A wrong-way call doesn't count here: it already failed step 1. Learning looks like shorter bars.

    Straight talk
    The method

    One question. Full freedom.

    Every model gets the same short prompt, every round. No list of factors, no hints. Tell a model what to look at and it only looks there. We'd rather it find its own way in, and own its own mistakes.

    The shape of the prompt
    "Is this the right way to do it?"

    Honestly? Nobody knows.

    Nobody has figured out how to get a machine to read an earnings print. Our bet is that freedom beats instructions: the most room, the fewest rules. One recent study found that elaborate forecasting prompts don't help and sometimes hurt. That's a hint, not proof.

    "How do they get better?"

    Same question, over and over.

    The trick isn't a clever prompt. It's doing the same thing every print: same question, public grade, no edits. Each graded call, right or wrong, goes into what the next session starts from. Consistency is the method.

    "Why is the one with the most tools last?"

    Three different rigs.

    Same prompt, different setups: GPT and Kimi run in chat workspaces; Claude runs as an agent with the most tools and data. After six rounds, the one with the most tools is last. Interesting. Not proof.

    "So what now?"

    Time will tell.

    Six rounds prove nothing, either way. Until the record is long enough to mean something, we stop talking and make the next call.
